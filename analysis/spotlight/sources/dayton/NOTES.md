The PDFs are kept locally in this folder and are not committed (they are copyrighted, and they are gitignored). Eric reports that most older DDN URLs now return 404, including the "Lasting Scars" pages, the redlining story, the jobs tracker and the 2017 opioid story.

## 1. Thomas Gnau, "Closure of GM-Moraine plant ended local manufacturing era 15 years ago," Dayton Daily News, Dec. 21, 2023
https://daytondailynews.com/local/closure-of-gm-moraine-plant-ended-local-manufacturing-era-15-years-ago/IJKOIX7MH5D2ZBBNC2ALBKOEC4/

- **Frigidaire:** the appliance plant ran on the site from 1951 to 1979.
- **Peak:** "At its peak, 4,300 people worked at GM-Moraine." The plant employed about 4,100 until mid-2006, when GM cut a third shift.
- **June 3, 2008:** GM announced it would close the plant. "About 2,500 people were employed at the Moraine complex" at that time.
- **Dec. 23, 2008:** the last SUV rolled off the line. "1,100 workers are left jobless by the plant closure" (sidebar). The article text says "about 1,000 people immediately lost their jobs," and "about half of Moraine's municipal revenue vanished."
- **NCR:** the closure came six months before NCR announced it was leaving Dayton for the Atlanta area.
- **What this settles:** the conflict between Wikipedia's 2,400 and the triage's DDN 1,100. Wikipedia's figure matches the June 2008 headcount (about 2,500), not the number at closing. Use 4,300 at peak, about 2,500 at the June 2008 announcement, and 1,100 at closing.
- **Location:** the plant is in Moraine, outside West Dayton. It is a regional cut, shown with county data.

## 2. Jordan Laird, "Deconstructing segregation: How highways contributed, and what can be done about it today," Dayton Daily News, May 2, 2021
https://www.daytondailynews.com/local/deconstructing-segregation-how-highways-contributed-and-what-can-be-done-about-it-today/LIAY4RKK3JBSPCLZ6XAQPRZZNA/

- **The Nickel:** in 1940, West Fifth Street between Bank Street and Broadway Street held "more than 70 businesses." It was a Black business district nicknamed "the Nickel."
- **I-75 and US-35:** Andrew Walsh (Sinclair Community College librarian, author of "Lost Dayton, Ohio") is quoted: "The construction of I-75 and U.S. 35 meant the demolition of many homes and businesses in multiple west side neighborhoods." I-75 (1960s) cut West Fifth off from downtown; it now ends at Mound Street.
- **1979 reports:** the city's report to the federal government predicted that I-675 would pull businesses and wealthy residents away from Dayton. The city asked for mitigation, including building US-35 farther west. The Miami Valley Regional Planning Commission also recommended nine mitigation steps, mostly fair housing across the region. "Few of the actions were taken."
- **I-675 approvals:** in 1979 the federal government approved I-675 only as far as US-35. In 1981 it approved the rest.
- **Downtown jobs:** a city planner estimates that a quarter to a third of downtown jobs left between 1985 and 1994, many to the I-675 corridor. This is an estimate, not a count.
- **Income shift:** an MVRPC and Kirwan Institute report found that from 1980 to 2010 the top tier of aggregate income moved outward along I-675 and I-75, while the lowest tier stayed in the urban core.
- **What it does not give:** the count of 283 homes, 17 businesses and 5 nonprofits for the US-35 West stretch. That still needs the 1990-92 DDN coverage or ODOT records.
- **Balance:** the article includes a dissent (Randal O'Toole, Cato Institute) that highways were not the cause of suburban flight. Note it if the story leans on the highway chapter.
