## Section 3 claims checked

1. "The City's own plans make the first link." **VERIFIED** as framing. The Miami Chapel plan (p. 19), the Vision (p. 2) and the Westwood plan (p. 6) all tie West Dayton's decline to redlining. The Westwood plan calls it a community "devastated by prolonged redlining, predatory lending and decades of disinvestment."
2. "Redlining and segregation packed Black families into West Dayton by the 1950s, and the area held little but older homes and public housing such as DeSoto Bass." **CHANGED.** The first half is supported (Miami Chapel p. 19; Vision p. 2). The second half is not: the plans describe "flourishing African-American small businesses" and Pineview, built in the 1950s by Black middle-income families. Suggested: "Starting in the 1950s, redlining left West Dayton as one of the few places in the city where Black families could buy homes, and DeSoto Bass was for years the only complex that would rent to them."
3. "A 1966 DDN article listed the lack of home loans as one grievance before the unrest." **UNSUPPORTED** in this pass. It is known only through Wikipedia's summary of DDN "Lasting Scars, Part 2," which is now 404. See pull list.
4. "Afterward many west side businesses moved or never reopened, according to the DDN series as summarized on Wikipedia." **CHANGED.** Source swap to *Dayton Jewish Observer*, Aug. 25, 2016: "Many merchants never came back." Kerner (1968) confirms two more disorders in 1967.
5. "The City says the US-35 freeway, finished in 1996, cut West Dayton in half and removed hundreds of homes along one stretch." **CHANGED.** "Cut West Dayton in half" is VERIFIED (Vision, Foreword: "bisected by the construction of U.S. 35"). "Finished in 1996" is PULL (DDN, Oct. 20 and 26, 1996). "Hundreds of homes along one stretch" is not from the City; it is from the Dayton Vistas blog. Suggested until the pull: "The City says the US-35 freeway split West Dayton into northern and southern halves, and building it took down many homes and businesses (Laird, DDN, 2021)."
6. "It also says the loss of thousands of factory jobs left West Dayton with more poverty and joblessness than the rest of the city." **CHANGED** (small). The Vision p. 2 says "Dayton lost thousands of manufacturing jobs and businesses," and that the result is "a higher percentage of families living below the poverty line, higher unemployment ... in West Dayton than most of the rest of the city." Change "the rest of the city" to "most of the rest of the city."
7. "For the later cuts (Delphi and GM closings in 2008, NCR leaving in 2009, the overdose rise, and the hospital closing in 2018), no source we found ties one to the next, so we can only say the timing lines up with the rise in vacancy (about 10% in 1990 to about 25% in 2008-2012) in our census numbers." **CHANGED.** Vacancy is VERIFIED in `analysis/spotlight/history_long.csv` (U047: 10.17 in 1990, 25.47 in ACS 2008-2012). GM Moraine 2008 is VERIFIED (Gnau), and so are the hospital closing in 2018 (WYSO) and the overdose rise (CDC). Two dates need a fix: NCR *announced* the move in June 2009 (SEC 10-Q), and the Delphi Home Avenue closing year is not confirmed (only the 2007 agreement to sell or close it). Suggested: "(the Delphi Home Avenue wind-down agreed in 2007, GM Moraine's closing in 2008, NCR's 2009 decision to leave, ...)". Gnau also notes that Moraine closed "six months before NCR announced it was leaving Dayton," which is a timing link, not a causal one.
