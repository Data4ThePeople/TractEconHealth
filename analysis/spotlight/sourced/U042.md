# U042 sourced timeline: Franklin Heights, Park West and Arlington Heights (ZIP 53206), north side of Milwaukee

**Summary:** All 20 entries now rest on a source I opened; 7 rest on tier A records and 13 on tier B. The biggest fixes: on the four kept tracts the area went from 72% to 95% Black between 1970 and 1980 (not 48% to 84%, which was the eight-tract figure), and Levine's 2017 number is 46% of employed prime-age adults working full time, not "less than half of adults."

Sourcing pass done 2026-11-19. HOLC shares were recomputed from the Mapping Inequality GeoJSON overlaid on 2020 TIGER tracts. Census shares come from analysis/spotlight/history_final.csv (the four kept tracts).

## Section 3 claims checked

1. "In the 1930s these blocks were a white, mostly German working-class area that federal appraisers rated 'C,' a step above the red 'D' zones to the south." **CHANGED.** The C grade is supported (71% of land, Mapping Inequality overlay). "White, mostly German" in the 1930s has no source; the only source (WUWM 2008) speaks of European ancestry about 1958. One strip (D6, in tract 8800) was graded D. Suggested: "In the late 1930s federal appraisers graded most of these blocks 'C,' or declining, one step above the red 'D' zones."
2. "In the 1960s freeway and urban renewal clearance took more than 8,000 homes and businesses near Walnut Street..." **VERIFIED** as attributed (TCLF: "over 8,000 homeowners and businesses"; Encyclopedia of Milwaukee for Bronzeville and Walnut Street). No primary count found.
3. "...and the WUWM and TCLF accounts say many displaced Black families moved north into this area." **CHANGED.** Only WUWM says this ("Many moved farther north into the 206 zip code"); TCLF does not say where people went. Cite WUWM alone.
4. "...our census numbers show it went from 48% to 84% Black between 1970 and 1980." **CHANGED.** On the four kept tracts: 72% to 95% (history_final.csv). 48% to 84% was the eight-tract figure.
5. "Marc Levine of UW-Milwaukee says Black men here were tied to factory work (about 55% of employed Black men in the 1970s) and that the factory losses of the 1980s hit Black neighborhoods 'much, much' harder." **VERIFIED** with a small fix: Levine's 55% is for employed Black men in Milwaukee, not "here." His quote: "the hit was much, much stronger in a black community" (WUWM, March 8, 2017).
6. "The A.O. Smith frame plant inside the area went from a reported 10,000 jobs to 300 at its 2006 closing." **VERIFIED** as attributed (10,000 is Howard Snyder's figure as reported by WUWM; 300 from Industrial Info, Oct. 10, 2005). By 2006 the plant was Tower Automotive's, not A.O. Smith's; say "the former A.O. Smith frame plant."
7. "The timing of job loss lines up with the rise in state imprisonment documented by Pawasarat and Quinn..." **CHANGED.** Pawasarat and Quinn document 1990-2012 and say Wisconsin's prison population "more than tripled since 1990." The factory job figures cover 1977-1987. The two periods follow each other but do not overlap, and the report does not compare them. Suggested: "The factory losses came first; the rise in state imprisonment that Pawasarat and Quinn document came in the 1990s and 2000s."
8. "...who argue that prison records then cut men off from work, for example through suspended driver's licenses." **CHANGED.** The report calls prison time "the most serious barrier to employment" and treats driver's license problems as a second barrier, often from unpaid fines, not as a result of prison records. Suggested: "who found that a prison record was the biggest barrier to getting hired, and that many men also could not legally drive to jobs because their licenses were suspended, often for unpaid fines."
9. "Their report makes that link; for the step from jobs to prison, this file only says the timing lines up." **UNSUPPORTED** as a story sentence; it is a note to the editor. Drop it from the story.
