Tract 55079004700 holds the former A.O. The plant site is not housing, so it does not drive resident numbers, and it is the center of the job-loss chapter.

Note: the four recommended tracts are only the western part of ZIP 53206, which runs east to about I-43. ZIP-level figures describe a larger area than the tracts.

## 3. How the cuts compound

In the 1930s these blocks were a white, mostly German working-class area that federal appraisers rated "C," a step above the red "D" zones to the south. In the 1960s freeway and urban renewal clearance took more than 8,000 homes and businesses near Walnut Street, and the WUWM and TCLF accounts say many displaced Black families moved north into this area; our census numbers show it went from 48% to 84% Black between 1970 and 1980. Marc Levine of UW-Milwaukee says Black men here were tied to factory work (about 55% of employed Black men in the 1970s) and that the factory losses of the 1980s hit Black neighborhoods "much, much" harder. The A.O. Smith frame plant inside the area went from a reported 10,000 jobs to 300 at its 2006 closing. The timing of job loss lines up with the rise in state imprisonment documented by Pawasarat and Quinn, who argue that prison records then cut men off from work, for example through suspended driver's licenses. Their report makes that link; for the step from jobs to prison, this file only says the timing lines up.

## 5. What makes it distinct

This is the place where Milwaukee's biggest auto-frame plant, once reported at 10,000 jobs, sat in the middle of a neighborhood that filled with families pushed north by freeway clearance just before the factory jobs left. It is also the ZIP code that UW-Milwaukee researchers called "ground zero" for the imprisonment of Black men in Wisconsin, with prison data that can be shown by ZIP.

## 6. Counter-evidence and caveats

- **ZIP 53206 is only partly covered.** Half the candidate tracts are not in 53206 at all. Even the four I keep are just the western part of the ZIP. ZIP-level incarceration and jobs numbers must be labeled as ZIP figures.
- **Redlining is weak here.** Only 9% of the recommended land was graded D; 71% was C. The red "D" story belongs to the old inner core to the south and east. The story should say the area was rated "declining," not "redlined."
- **The "most incarcerated ZIP in America" line is not supported.** PolitiFact (2019) found 53206 first in Wisconsin by count but fourth by rate, and no Wisconsin ZIP in a national top 40.
- **Freeway clearance was not in these tracts.** I-43 and the Park West corridor are south and east. The link to this area runs through where displaced families moved, which the sources state in general terms only.
- **The triage note's "47% of manufacturing jobs lost, 1979-2000" could not be found** in the cited Milwaukee Independent column. That column gives 91,400 (1977) and 63,900 (1987) instead, from "the Department of Labor," without a table.
- **Allis-Chalmers was in West Allis**, a separate city. It is part of the regional job story but should not be described as a neighborhood employer. I could not confirm its job counts.
- **History numbers need a rerun.** history_long.csv covers all 8 candidate tracts. It must be rebuilt on the 4 recommended tracts before charts are made.
- **Odd values in history_long.csv (8 tracts):** unemployment drops from 30.2% (ACS 2008-2012) to 13.1% (ACS 2020-2024), a sharp fall that may reflect the strong 2022-2024 labor market or ACS sampling error at this size; check margins of error. Vacancy is 15.5% in the 2020 census but 23.9% in ACS 2020-2024; the two sources measure at different times and the gap is large enough to check. The cluster life expectancy row has fit 0.727, under 0.8, so I did not use it; the four recommended tracts all have their own USALEEP values (listed above).
- **1970 starting point.** Our tract series begins in 1970, when the area was already 48% Black and in transition. The 1950s-1960s change happens before the chart starts.
