# U063: Woodlawn and the west edge of Washington Park, Chicago

Deep research, November 19, 2026. Triage notes in `research/U063.md` were checked line by line. The triage HOLC overlay is confirmed. The biggest addition: the 1928 covenant at the center of *Hansberry v. Lee* covered exactly two of our tracts (4205 and 4206), the Hansberry house is in tract 4206, and the HOLC appraiser's own 1939 notes for that area quote the court ruling that upheld the covenant. Several triage items (the South Campus strip, the Green Line demolition, and the fast price rise near the Obama Center) are real but sit just east of Cottage Grove Avenue, outside our tracts. Section 6 covers that.

## 1. Boundary

**Local names.** West Woodlawn (Chicago community area 42), west of Cottage Grove Avenue from 60th Street south to 71st Street, and the south end of the Washington Park community area (40), 60th to 63rd Street. The north half of the Woodlawn part, 60th to 63rd between Cottage Grove and King Drive (once South Park Avenue), is the old "Washington Park Subdivision" of the Hansberry case.

**Method.** Community areas from the City of Chicago boundary file (https://data.cityofchicago.org/resource/igwz-8jzy.geojson) overlaid on 2020 cartographic tract shapes (project copy `data/raw/tiger/cb_2020_us_tract_500k.shp`). 2020 population, group quarters and homes from the 2020 Census redistricting file (https://api.census.gov/data/2020/dec/pl). Assisted housing from HUD's Public Housing Developments and Multifamily Properties layers (https://services.arcgis.com/VTyQ9soqVukalItT/arcgis/rest/services). The Hansberry house, 6140 S. Rhodes Ave., was placed with the Census geocoder (https://geocoding.geo.census.gov): it falls in tract **17031420600**, one of ours.

## 2. Timeline

1. **February 1, 1928.** A racial covenant was recorded for about 27 blocks between 60th and 63rd Streets and between Cottage Grove and South Park (now King Drive) Avenues, covering about 500 owners. It barred sale, lease or occupancy by Black people until January 1, 1948, and took effect only if owners of 95% of the frontage signed. That area is our tracts 4205 and 4206. *Lee v. Hansberry*, 372 Ill. 369 (Ill. 1939), https://static.case.law/ill/372/cases/0369-01.json ; Seligman, "Woodlawn," *Encyclopedia of Chicago*, http://www.encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org/pages/1378.html
2. **1928 context.** The *Encyclopedia of Chicago* says local businessmen and University of Chicago officials organized to keep Black residents from moving east through the Washington Park Subdivision, and that "local landlords agreed to a joint restrictive covenant." The Chicago Real Estate Board had campaigned in 1927 to spread covenants across the South Side. Seligman, "Woodlawn," as above ; Hirsch, "Restrictive Covenants," *Encyclopedia of Chicago*, http://www.encyclopedia.chicagohistory.org/pages/1067.html
3. **1933 to 1947.** The University of Chicago spent $110,923.27 on "community interests," $83,597.46 of it apparently on defending covenants, according to Arnold Hirsch. The Woodlawn Property Owners' League was funded one-half by the University, which paid about $2,250 of the League's costs in the Hansberry litigation (University President's Office records). Hagerty, "White Minds, Red Lines" (B.A. thesis, University of Chicago, 2019), pp. 1, 37, citing Hirsch, *Making the Second Ghetto*, p. 145, and Hutchins Administration Records, Box 58, Folder 6, https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/records/6zdg8-1ja88
4. **May and June 1937.** The Hansberry family moved into 6140 S. Rhodes Ave. in May 1937, buying through an attorney because of the covenant. On June 3, 1937, Anna Lee, a member of the Woodlawn Property Owners Association, sued to remove them. City of Chicago, Commission on Chicago Landmarks, *Lorraine Hansberry House* landmark report (2009), https://www.chicago.gov/dam/city/depts/zlup/Historic_Preservation/Publications/Lorraine_Hansberry_House_Landmark_Report.pdf . The house is in 2020 tract 17031420600 (Census geocoder).
5. **October 10, 1939.** The Illinois Supreme Court upheld the covenant, ruling the Hansberrys were bound by an earlier case. *Lee v. Hansberry*, 372 Ill. 369, as above.
6. **October 1939 to January 1940.** The Home Owners' Loan Corporation graded all five tracts "D" (red): 97% to 100% of tracts 4008, 4205, 4206 and 4207, and 75% of tract 4212 (computed for this note from Mapping Inequality polygons). The appraiser's notes for area D78, the covenant area, are dated October 1, 1939, and quote the court ruling: "It is expected ultimately that this entire area will revert to the Colored race." The D74 notes, covering tract 4008, say "No mortgage money available north of 63rd, between Cottage Grove and Halsted." Mapping Inequality, Chicago areas D74, D75, D78, https://dsl.richmond.edu/panorama/redlining/map/IL/Chicago/area_descriptions/D78
7. **November 12, 1940.** In *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32, the U.S. Supreme Court reversed. The trial court had found that owners of "only about 54 per cent of the frontage" had signed, not 95%. The ruling turned on due process, not on whether covenants were legal. https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/311/32 ; https://static.case.law/us/311/cases/0032-01.json
8. **May 3, 1948.** In *Shelley v. Kraemer*, 334 U.S. 1, the Supreme Court made racial covenants unenforceable in court. Hirsch writes that in Chicago it mostly cleared "the legal clouds shadowing property already falling into black hands." https://static.case.law/us/334/cases/0001-01.json ; Hirsch, "Restrictive Covenants," as above
9. **1930 to 1960.** Woodlawn (community area) went from 66,052 people, 13.0% Black, to 81,279 people, 89.1% Black. By 1960 it had "deteriorating, crowded housing." Seligman, "Woodlawn," census table, as above
10. **1960 to 1964.** After the 1959 federal Housing Act's Section 112, the University turned to Woodlawn in 1960 with a plan for a "South Campus" on the strip between 60th and 61st Streets. The Woodlawn Organization (TWO), formed in 1961 by local pastors and block clubs with Saul Alinsky's Industrial Areas Foundation, fought it. In 1964 TWO won a written promise that the campus "would not extend south of 61st Street." The strip lies east of Cottage Grove, next to but outside our tracts. Bradley, "The University of Chicago, Urban Renewal, and the Black Community," *Black Perspectives* (AAIHS), April 12, 2021, https://www.aaihs.org/the-university-of-chicago-urban-renewal-and-the-black-community/ ; Gibson, "Due South," *University of Chicago Magazine*, February 2006, https://magazine.uchicago.edu/0602/features/south.shtml ; Seligman, as above
11. **1968 to 1971.** After the April 1968 assassination of Martin Luther King Jr., most white business owners left Woodlawn, and "a rash of arsons destroyed a reported 362 abandoned buildings." The University's magazine counts "insurance fires" that "claimed more than 100 buildings" in the 1960s and 1970s. Seligman, as above ; Gibson, as above
12. **1970 to 1990.** In our five tracts, population fell from 25,390 to 16,435 (35% lower). Poverty rose from 21.2% to 40.2%, and unemployment from 6.2% to 23.2% (19.1% by 1980). By 2020 population was 12,181, 52% below 1970. Woodlawn as a whole fell from 81,279 in 1960 to 27,473 in 1990. NHGIS tract data (`history_final.csv`), https://www.nhgis.org ; Seligman, census table, as above
13. **June 5, 1996.** The CTA board voted to back the city's plan to tear down the Green Line between Cottage Grove and Dorchester Avenues, which the city called "critical for residential redevelopment along 63rd Street." Riders on the branch had fallen from 12,600 in 1978 to 2,800 in 1993. Service ended at Cottage Grove, on the east edge of our tracts, and the structure east of it was taken down in 1997. Washburn, "CTA Board Backs Demolition of Green Line Woodlawn Leg," *Chicago Tribune*, June 6, 1996, reprinted at https://chicago-l.org/articles/woodlawn3.html . Demolition date (September 27, 1997, per https://www.chicago-l.org/operations/lines/jacksonpark.html): PULL the Tribune story.
14. **2010 to 2015, and 2023.** Life expectancy in the five tracts ranged from 72.0 to 74.7 years (USALEEP), against an average of 77.8 across Cook County tracts. In 2023, the Chicago Health Atlas put life expectancy at 73.2 in Woodlawn and 72.5 in Washington Park, against 82.6 in Hyde Park next door and 78.7 for Chicago. USALEEP, https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nvss/usaleep/usaleep.html (project copy `data/raw/usaleep/US_A.CSV`) ; Chicago Health Atlas, topic VRLE (IDPH death records), https://chicagohealthatlas.org/indicators/VRLE
15. **July 29, 2016 and September 9, 2020.** The Obama Foundation chose Jackson Park, east of Woodlawn, for the Obama Presidential Center. On September 9, 2020, the City Council passed a Woodlawn housing ordinance: $4.5 million for affordable housing programs, 30% affordable units on 52 city-owned lots, and a right of first refusal for tenants. A community benefits coalition had asked for more. Obama Foundation press release, July 29, 2016, https://www.obama.org/press-releases/obama-foundation-announces-jackson-park-site-obama-presidential-center/ ; Spielman, *Chicago Sun-Times*, September 9, 2020, https://chicago.suntimes.com/city-hall/2020/9/9/21428936/obama-presidential-center-jackson-park-residents-housing-community-gentrificaton-woodlawn
16. **June 2026 (newest chapter).** The Obama Presidential Center opened to the public on June 19, 2026, after a June 18 ceremony. Just before, the Illinois Answers Project reported that prices for single-family and multi-family homes on Woodlawn's east side had doubled since 2019 (DePaul Institute for Housing Studies data). Only one project had been built on the 52 reserved lots. Forster, *Columbia Chronicle*, June 18, 2026, https://columbiachronicle.com/metro/chicago-welcomes-the-obama-presidential-center-after-years-of-planning/ ; Huang, King and Adams, "Woodlawn Residents Face Mounting Housing Costs as Obama Center Nears Opening," Illinois Answers Project and *South Side Weekly*, June 5, 2026, https://ihs-website-v2-production.s3.amazonaws.com/filer_public/8d/65/8d652992-9089-45a7-8cb4-4aaf0c79a941/2026-06-08_south_side_weekly.pdf

## 3. How the cuts compound

The *Encyclopedia of Chicago* says University officials and local businessmen organized to keep Black families out of the Washington Park Subdivision, and the 1928 covenant followed. University records show it paid part of the cost of defending that covenant in court. The federal appraiser who graded the area in 1939 described the coming racial change and cited the court ruling. The neighboring D74 notes say no mortgage money was available north of 63rd Street. The 1940 Supreme Court case opened the subdivision to Black families, but Hirsch writes that the end of covenants mostly confirmed changes already under way. The *Encyclopedia* says many of Woodlawn's new residents had been pushed out by redevelopment elsewhere, that most white business owners left after 1968, and that arson destroyed hundreds of empty buildings from 1968 to 1971. The *Chicago Tribune* reported in 1996 that ridership on the 63rd Street branch fell by about 78% from 1978 to 1993, and that the city wanted to tear down the stretch east of Cottage Grove to clear land for housing. For the drop in population and the low life expectancy in these five tracts, the timing lines up, but no source here ties them directly to the covenant or the HOLC grade.

## 5. What makes it distinct

This is the one place in the series where the covenant, the court case and the HOLC grade meet on the same blocks: the 1928 covenant area, the *Hansberry v. Lee* house, and the appraiser's "D" notes that quote the court ruling all fall in tracts 4205 and 4206. A large university next door shows up in the record at each stage, from paying for the covenant's defense to the South Campus fight, and the Obama Center now brings new investment pressure a few blocks east.

## 6. Counter-evidence and caveats

- **Several headline events are just east of our tracts.** The South Campus strip (60th to 61st, Cottage Grove to Stony Island), the Green Line stretch torn down in 1997 (Cottage Grove to Dorchester), and the fastest price rise (East Woodlawn) are all east of Cottage Grove. Green Line service still runs to Cottage Grove, at the east edge of our tracts. Say "next door" or "in Woodlawn," not "in these tracts."
- **The covenant failed in court, and it was not the only force.** *Hansberry v. Lee* opened the subdivision in 1940. Hirsch writes that Depression and wartime housing shortages "probably did more to freeze Chicago's residential patterns than did the covenants." The story should not claim the covenant alone made the area poor.
- **The HOLC grade followed racial change.** The D78 notes describe Black families already moving in. As with every HOLC map, the grade recorded and reinforced lending patterns; it did not start them.
- **University funding figures are second-hand here.** The $110,923 and $83,597 figures are Hirsch's, quoted in a University of Chicago B.A. thesis that cites the University's own archives. Eric should pull Hirsch, p. 145, before publishing the numbers. The thesis calls the support financial; Plotkin's 2001 article (not opened) is cited by others for the word "covert."
- **Name of the owners' group.** Sources use both "Woodlawn Property Owners' Association" and "League." The thesis says the Association was reorganized as the League in 1937. Use "Woodlawn Property Owners' Association" for 1928 to 1937.
- **Arson count is "reported."** The 362 figure comes from the *Encyclopedia* with no primary record cited. Use "a reported 362."
- **Contract buying is not documented for these tracts.** Beryl Satter's *Family Properties* centers on the West Side (North Lawndale). The D74 HOLC notes mention a "contract broker" with 500 waiting Black buyers in 1939, but I found no source on contract sales in these five tracts. Leave contract buying out or frame it citywide.
- **Population loss started before 1970.** Our tract series begins in 1970; the 1960 peak is a community-area number, not a tract number. Do not mix them in one chart line.
- **Reinvestment and displacement pressure now.** The Obama Center opened June 19, 2026. East Woodlawn prices doubled from 2019 to 2025, and the Illinois Answers Project found less than a third of local housing affordable in 2024, half the share of 15 years earlier. The 2020 ordinance has produced one project on the reserved lots. Our west-side tracts have not shown the same price jump in the sources found. The story should present this as an open question, not a finished outcome.
- **Health gap is wide but not tied to history by any source.** The 9.4-year gap between Woodlawn and Hyde Park (2023) is real. No source here links it causally to the covenant or HOLC grade.
- **Income and health are less extreme than Englewood.** U063 sits at the 7.8 income and 5.5 health percentiles.
