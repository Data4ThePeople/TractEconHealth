## 3. How the cuts compound

The sources draw a straight line through the first half of this history. The Real Estate Exchange backed the 1916 segregation vote; when the courts blocked it, the Exchange wrote the standard covenant and drew "unrestricted zones" that held most Black residents in the near north side (Gordon 2009, pp. 6-10; Rothstein 2014). Gordon says the federal residential security maps "closely followed the contours" of the Black community and this ring of covenants, and that the local FHA office followed those ratings until 1962. He also says city zoning "underzoned" the north side, which made its homes ineligible for FHA help (pp. 14, 18). So mortgage money went to the county instead: 62,772 FHA loans there against 12,166 in the city from 1934 to 1960. Urban renewal then pushed more families into the same area. Gordon writes that displaced families from Mill Creek moved mostly "west and north," with "the effect of both deepening segregation" and "creating demands for clearance" in the neighborhoods that took them in (p. 28). The city's own JeffVanderLou history says the neighborhood became "a target for housing clearance for projects like Pruitt-Igoe." Gordon calls the 1979 hospital closing "a major blow" to the local institutions and the Black middle class, and says commercial buildings in The Ville and Greater Ville were boarded up by the 1980s (p. 63). For the NGA West site, the timing lines up: the federal impact statement found the site mostly vacant land by 2015, after decades of population loss, but no source we read says the earlier history caused the site choice. In our tract data, the timing also lines up: population fell by more than three-quarters from 1970 to 2020 while poverty stayed above 37%.

## 5. What makes it distinct

The house at the center of the Supreme Court case that ended court enforcement of racial covenants stands inside these tracts, so the redlining story here is not just a map grade but a court record about a real block. The same few square miles then took in families displaced by Mill Creek clearance, sat across the street from Pruitt-Igoe, lost the hospital next door, and gave up nearly a quarter of one tract to a federal campus.

## 6. Counter-evidence and caveats

- **Our tracts miss the landmarks.** The Ville proper, Homer G. Phillips Hospital and the Pruitt-Igoe site are in adjacent tracts we cannot add. The story must say "next to" for these.
- **The Greater Ville was not redlined in red.** Tracts 110100 and 110300 were mostly graded C and B in 1937, and the Shelley house was in a C area. The western half of the story is about covenants and the Exchange's zones, not a D grade. Only the eastern tracts (121200, 127100) were nearly all D.
- **Grades may record existing patterns as well as shape them.** Gordon says ratings "closely followed" where Black residents already lived. We cannot separate how much the map caused later disinvestment from what it recorded.
- **Sources differ on counts.** Covenants: about 300 by 1945 (Rothstein) vs. almost 380 by the 1940s (Gordon). 1916 vote: "3-to-1" (STLPR) vs. "two-thirds" (city equity page). Eminent domain: 46 properties (Lincoln) vs. 44 buildings (WashU humanities, citing the Post-Dispatch).
- **Missouri Supreme Court date.** The state archives timeline says the Missouri Supreme Court upheld the covenant in 1945. The U.S. opinion shows the suit was filed Oct. 9, 1945, so a 1945 state supreme court ruling is unlikely. Use the U.S. opinion's dates.
- **The 1916 ordinance never took real effect.** Gordon says it "sat in legal limbo for about a year" until Buchanan. Wikipedia, citing Primm, says an NAACP suit won an injunction in April 1916 (to pull).
- **Mill Creek destinations are general.** Gordon says most moved "west and north." No source we read counts how many moved into these five tracts.
- **Pruitt-Igoe's decline is debated.** Architectural Record and the 2011 film argue the "myth" that tenants caused it. Avoid blaming design or residents.
- **Homer G. Phillips' closing was about more than race.** BlackPast cites "a wide range of complex issues." Outpatient care continued, and the building reopened as senior housing (2003 per the city, 2004 per BlackPast).
- **NGA West displaced few people relative to the site.** The impact statement excerpt says 76% of parcels were vacant land and 13% were occupied homes. One resident quoted by STLPR was glad to move. The count of households moved is UNCONFIRMED.
- **Resident-led rebuilding.** Jeff-Vander-Lou, Inc. (founded 1966 by Macler C. Shepard) built affordable housing for more than a decade, per the city. The story should include it.
- **History numbers need rebuilding.** `history_final.csv` covers 8 tracts, including two in Illinois and 126600. The 1970 and 1980 figures are map overlays; 1990 and 2000 are crosswalk estimates. Unemployment in the 8-tract set fell to 9.9% in 2020-2024 while poverty rose to 47.7%.
- **HOLC area descriptions** for St. Louis areas D9, D10, D6 and C13 were not available from the Mapping Inequality site in this session.
