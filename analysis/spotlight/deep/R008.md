## 1. Boundary

Navajo local government is organized by chapters. ## 2. Timeline

1. **December 16, 1882. Executive order** set aside about 2.5 million acres in Arizona for the Hopi "and such other Indians" as the Interior Secretary might settle there ([CRS, IF12953](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IF12953.html); date in [P.L. 93-531](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-88/pdf/STATUTE-88-Pg1712.pdf)). The "other Indians" wording is UNCONFIRMED in this pass.
2. **June 14, 1934. Act expanding the Navajo Reservation** so that it surrounded the 1882 area ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IF12953.html)).
3. **1962. Healing v. Jones.** A federal court gave the Hopi exclusive rights to about 631,000 acres and made about 1.9 million acres a Joint Use Area shared equally ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IF12953.html)).
4. **1964 and 1966. Peabody coal contracts.** Peabody signed with the Navajo Tribe in 1964 and the Hopi Tribe in 1966. The lawyer who negotiated for the Hopi, John Boyden, was also being paid by Peabody, according to reporting by the Phoenix New Times (1997) ([Wikipedia, citing that reporting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Mesa_Peabody_Coal_controversy)). Exact lease dates and royalty rates: UNCONFIRMED.
5. **1965 to 2005. Groundwater pumping.** Industrial and town use of the N-aquifer rose from about 70 acre-feet in 1965 to 7,330 acre-feet in 2005. Peabody used about 70% to 75% of the total through the mid-1980s and about 60% from then to 2005. In the confined part of the aquifer, monitoring wells dropped 80 to 130 feet by 2006 ([USGS Black Mesa monitoring program](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/arizona-water-science-center/science/black-mesa-monitoring-program)). The 273-mile slurry line used an average of 3 million gallons a day ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Mesa_Peabody_Coal_controversy)).
6. **1966 to 2009. Bennett Freeze.** The Commissioner of Indian Affairs barred building and repairs on about 1.5 million acres in the western former Joint Use Area. Congress wrote it into law in 1980 (P.L. 96-305) and repealed it in 2009 (P.L. 111-18). In 2022 the Navajo Nation reported that 24% of homes in the area were livable and almost 60% lacked electricity ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IF12953.html); [P.L. 111-18](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-111publ18/pdf/PLAW-111publ18.pdf)). Nearly 10,000 people lived in the area during the freeze ([Los Angeles Times, 2009](https://www.latimes.com/la-na-bennet-freeze-5-2009nov5-story.html)). **The freeze area lies mainly west of the recommended tracts (Tuba City, Cameron); whether any part of these 5 tracts was frozen is UNCONFIRMED.**
7. **1974 to 1976. Navajo Generating Station** units came online, fed by coal from the Kayenta Mine over a 90-mile rail line ([IEEFA, May 2019, p. 2](https://ieefa.org/sites/default/files/resources/Imminent-Public-Budget-Crisis-Confronts-Hopi-Navajo-Tribes-With-Coal-Economy-Collapse_May-2019.pdf)).
8. **December 22, 1974. Navajo-Hopi Land Settlement Act (P.L. 93-531, 88 Stat. 1712).** It ordered mediation and then partition of the Joint Use Area and created a relocation commission ([statute](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-88/pdf/STATUTE-88-Pg1712.pdf); [CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IF12953.html)).
9. **1977. Partition.** After mediation failed, a court split the Joint Use Area into Hopi Partitioned Lands and Navajo Partitioned Lands ([CRS](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IF12953.html)). Which of the 5 tracts include partition-line land (Hard Rock, Piñon and Low Mountain are the likely ones) is UNCONFIRMED.
10. **Through December 2017. Relocation count.** The Office of Navajo and Hopi Indian Relocation had moved more than 3,600 Navajo and Hopi families, about 16,700 people. The office was set to close by September 30, 2026 ([CRS, updated October 14, 2026](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IF12953.html)). Counts by chapter: UNCONFIRMED. (Older estimates of "6,000" or "12,000" Navajo people relocated are not used here.)
11. **December 31, 2005. Mohave Generating Station and Black Mesa Mine close.** The Nevada plant shut under a settlement of a Clean Air Act lawsuit, and the slurry line stopped ([High Country News](https://www.hcn.org/40years/blog/the-end-of-the-mojave-coal-fired-power-plant/)). MSHA records show the Black Mesa Mine produced 3.88 million short tons in 2005 with an average of 183 workers (mine, plant and office), and none in 2006. Peabody's water use fell about 70% ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/arizona-water-science-center/science/black-mesa-monitoring-program)). Lost tribal revenue from this closure: UNCONFIRMED.
12. **2016. Hopi budget.** $16.8 million of the Hopi Tribe's $21.1 million budget came from coal-related revenue ([IEEFA, p. 4, citing Hopi Tutuveni](https://ieefa.org/sites/default/files/resources/Imminent-Public-Budget-Crisis-Confronts-Hopi-Navajo-Tribes-With-Coal-Economy-Collapse_May-2019.pdf)).
13. **August 2019. Kayenta Mine stops mining** ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/arizona-water-science-center/science/black-mesa-monitoring-program)). MSHA records show 6.55 million short tons and an average of 416 workers in 2018, 3.84 million tons in 2019, and zero output from 2020 on. A 2012 federal lab report said 93% of mine workers were tribal members ([NREL, cited in Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navajo_Generating_Station); report link https://www.nrel.gov/docs/fy12osti/53024.pdf did not load in this pass, UNCONFIRMED).
14. **November 18, 2019. Navajo Generating Station stops generating** ([Navajo-Hopi Observer](https://www.nhonews.com/news/2019/nov/18/navajo-generating-station-shuts-down-permanently/)). Navajo Nation leaders projected revenue falling from $172 million (fiscal 2019) to $132 million in fiscal 2021, a 23% cut. The plant and mine together had about 700 jobs ([IEEFA, pp. 1-3](https://ieefa.org/sites/default/files/resources/Imminent-Public-Budget-Crisis-Confronts-Hopi-Navajo-Tribes-With-Coal-Economy-Collapse_May-2019.pdf)).
15. **2020. COVID-19.** In 23 states with good data, the COVID-19 case rate among American Indian and Alaska Native people was 594 per 100,000, about 250% higher than the rate among white people (169 per 100,000) ([CDC MMWR, August 28, 2020](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/69/wr/mm6934e1.htm)). Navajo Nation case and death totals: UNCONFIRMED (the Navajo Department of Health dashboard no longer shows them).

## 3. How the cuts compound

Federal land rulings in 1962, 1974 and 1977 divided the land around Black Mesa, and the federal relocation office moved more than 3,600 families. The Bennett Freeze blocked building in a large area to the west for 43 years; the CRS reports that most homes there are still unlivable or without power, but whether the freeze reached these 5 tracts is not confirmed. At the same time, coal mined on the mesa paid most of the Hopi Tribe's budget and, by its leaders' projections, about a quarter of the Navajo Nation's revenue. USGS reports that N-aquifer wells fell while pumping, mostly by Peabody, rose, and began to level off after the slurry line stopped; the timing lines up. When the mines closed in 2005 and 2019, IEEFA documents the budget cuts that followed; the tract data do not yet show an income drop, so for household income the timing lines up but no source here proves the link. Today a quarter of the homes in these tracts still lack complete plumbing.

## 5. What makes it distinct

Coal under the mesa paid tribal budgets and drew down the aquifer for 50 years, while federal land rulings moved thousands of families nearby, and then two mine closures in 2005 and 2019 took both the jobs and the revenue. Unlike most coal stories, the people who lived on the coal still lack running water in a quarter of their homes.

## 6. Counter-evidence and caveats

- **Weak health time series.** IHME data stop at the county, and Navajo and Apache counties include off-reservation towns. There is no long health series for these 5 tracts. The USALEEP tract life expectancy for the old cluster (75.2, 2010-2015) is higher than in Pine Ridge.
- **Tract boundaries shifted.** Stability back to 1990 is 0.646 for the old cluster, under our 0.8 rule. Our 1990 rows cannot be shown; the series really starts in 2000 and must be rebuilt for the 5 tracts.
- **Income has not clearly fallen after closures.** The old cluster's poverty rate fell from 47.4% (2000) to 42.8% (2008-12) and was 43.6% in 2020-24. The story is about lost tribal revenue and jobs, not a measured drop in household income.
- **Bennett Freeze may be outside the area.** If the editor wants the freeze in the story, the tract list may need to move west.
- **Census undercount.** The 2020 Post-Enumeration Survey found a 5.64% net undercount of American Indian and Alaska Native people on reservations (4.88% in 2010) ([Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2022/2020-census-estimates-of-undercount-and-overcount.html)). Many homes here have no street address, which makes counting harder (UNCONFIRMED as a cause).
- **Differential privacy (2020).** Statistical noise added to 2020 census counts is largest for small areas ([Census disclosure avoidance](https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/decennial-census/decade/2020/planning-management/process/disclosure-avoidance.html)). Our old cluster shows 25,171 people in the 2020 census but 19,679 in the ACS, a gap that needs checking before any population chart.
- **Two nations.** Coal revenue and the land dispute involve both the Navajo Nation and the Hopi Tribe. Even with the Hopi tract dropped, the story must describe the Hopi Tribe accurately.
