# U061: Englewood and Fuller Park, Chicago South Side (deep dive)

This file checks the triage notes (research/U061.md), fixes what was wrong, and sets the tract list.

Method notes for this pass:
- HOLC shares were recomputed here by overlaying the Mapping Inequality national GeoJSON (https://dsl.richmond.edu/panorama/redlining/static/mappinginequality.json) on the 2020 Census cartographic tracts, area-weighted.
- Community areas come from the City of Chicago boundary file (https://data.cityofchicago.org/resource/igwz-8jzy.geojson).
- 2020 population, group quarters, and block-level housing units come from the 2020 PL 94-171 file via the Census API.
- Homicide counts, the hardship index, and community area life expectancy come straight from the City of Chicago Data Portal.
- The Encyclopedia of Chicago site refused connections during this pass. Facts that rest on it are read through Wikipedia's citations and marked as secondhand. Web search was also unavailable for part of this pass.

**Recommended tract list (7):** 17031680900, 17031681000, 17031681200, 17031681300, 17031834600, 17031835500, 17031835600. The 2020 population is 15,520, and group quarters total 67 people.

**Drop: 17031340600.** One 2020 census block in this tract holds 972 of its 1,151 people (84%) and 379 of its 508 housing units. 3770 S. Wentworth Ave. geocodes to this tract, and that is where the Wentworth Gardens public housing complex stands. Wentworth Gardens opened in 1947 with 344 units (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wentworth_Gardens; unit count not rechecked). So one public housing complex drives this tract's numbers. It is also in a different community area (Armour Square) and holds the ballpark.

**Kept with notes:**
- **Tract 6809 (Kennedy-King College).** The campus covers 40 acres and has no on-campus housing (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kennedy%E2%80%93King_College). The tract's group quarters count is 11, so the college does not shape resident numbers. One block near 65th St. and Normal Ave. holds 304 housing units and 710 people (20% of the tract), likely one large apartment complex (name and type UNCONFIRMED).
- **Tract 8355 (Fuller Park).** One block holds 335 units and 311 people (20% of the tract). That about-one-person-per-unit pattern often means a senior building (UNCONFIRMED). It does not make up most of the tract.
- **Small numbers.** The two Fuller Park tracts hold only 2,482 people, so their ACS estimates are noisy (see Caveats).

**Crossing lines.** All seven kept tracts sit almost entirely in one community area each: five in Englewood (68) and two in Fuller Park (37). None crosses into another city. The story should name both places. They are next to each other, along the Dan Ryan, but their histories differ: Fuller Park is a highway and stockyard story, and Englewood is a story about a big shopping district emptying out.

**Our history numbers need a rerun** on the 7 tracts. Dropping Wentworth Gardens will slightly raise the owner share and lower poverty in recent years. It barely affects the 1970 to 1990 numbers (fit 0.987 to 0.997).

## 3. How the cuts compound

The Encyclopedia of Chicago, as cited on Wikipedia, makes the clearest chain for Fuller Park. The neighborhood was rated "hazardous" by HOLC. Then the Dan Ryan split it and displaced a large share of its people. Interstate trucking made the stockyards next door obsolete, and they closed in 1971. After that, no new housing was built for 35 years, and banks made fewer home improvement loans there than anywhere else in the city. For Englewood, the sources we found date each loss (the 1960 population peak, the loss of the 63rd and Halsted anchor stores after 1978, and the 2018 school closings), but they do not tie one to the next, so we can only say the timing lines up with the rise in unemployment (7.8% in 1970 to 27.6% in 1990) and the drop in population in our census numbers.

## 5. What makes it distinct

Fuller Park shows the clearest case in the series of an expressway and a closing stockyard squeezing a small neighborhood, which lost 85% of its people from 1950 to 2020. Englewood next door shows a 1960 shopping hub of nearly 100,000 people losing three of every four residents, with the 2018 high school closings coming on top of that.

## 6. Counter-evidence and caveats

- **Health improved even as the economy fell.** City life expectancy data show Englewood rising from 62.4 years (1990) to 70.7 (2010), and Fuller Park from 59.8 to 69.8. The gap to the city narrowed somewhat (Englewood from 8.1 to 7.1 years behind). The story should not say health kept getting worse; it should say the gap stayed large.
- **Triage claims that did not hold up.** The triage said Fuller Park had "the highest hardship score" and "40% unemployment," from a 2013 Chicago Magazine piece (the link now returns 404). The City's 2008-2012 data rank Fuller Park second (97, behind Riverdale at 98) with 33.9% unemployment. Use the City figures.
- **Secondhand sources.** The Dan Ryan "a third of its residents" claim, the 1969 to 2004 permit count, and the 1980s loan ranking all come from the Encyclopedia of Chicago through Wikipedia. The Encyclopedia site could not be reached this pass. Get the original page before publishing.
- **Englewood's 1980 numbers conflict.** Wikipedia's text gives 62,069 people and its table gives 59,075.
- **Reinvestment.** The $192 million Kennedy-King campus (2007), Englewood STEM High School (2019), and the 2010 to 2014 Englewood Flyover all came after the decline. A Whole Foods opened at 832 W. 63rd St. in 2016; that address geocodes to tract 8348, just outside the cluster (opening and later closing dates UNCONFIRMED). Fuller Park's Eden Place Nature Center (2003) turned a brownfield into a 3-acre nature site (https://www.chicagotribune.com/2011/04/25/eden-place-nature-center-serves-as-model-for-what-urban-communities-can-do-with-vacant-land/).
- **Race shift since 2010.** Our numbers show the cluster's Black share falling from 97% (2010) to 90% (2020). Wikipedia lists Englewood as 9.5% Hispanic in 2024. This is new and should be described, not explained.
- **Small-number noise.** Fuller Park tracts are small (898 and 1,584 people). Their ACS rates move a lot from year to year. Tract 8356 shows 66% poverty in ACS 2020-2024.
- **Homicide counts** are incidents in the Crimes dataset, not victims, and are by community area, not tract.
- **Overlap.** This is one of the most covered neighborhoods in the U.S. The triage notes it overlaps with the other Chicago seeds (Woodlawn U063, West Garfield Park U065). Pick one Chicago story.
