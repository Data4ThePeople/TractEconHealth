## 3. How the cuts compound

Central was where most Black newcomers to Cleveland settled after 1910, and federal appraisers in the late 1930s graded almost all of it "Hazardous." In the same years the federal government chose Central for its first public housing, and later estates, including King-Kennedy and the Cedar high-rise, were added nearby, so Black public housing tenants ended up concentrated in a few East Side estates, as a 1966 Civil Rights Commission report noted. In the 1950s and 1960s, city urban renewal displaced Black families from other areas, and the Encyclopedia of Cleveland History says many moved into Hough; after the 1966 Hough uprising, HUD cut the city's urban renewal funds, according to historian Leonard Moore as cited on Wikipedia. Central lost nearly half its people in the 1960s alone, and our census numbers show poverty and joblessness rising each decade through 1990 as county factory work shrank; the timing lines up, but no source I found ties a specific plant closing to Central. The 2007 foreclosure crisis centered on Slavic Village next door, though with only about 1 in 5 homes owner-occupied, Central had fewer mortgages to lose.

## 5. What makes it distinct

Central is where the federal government built its first public housing, inside a neighborhood its own appraisers had just graded "Hazardous," and later estates kept stacking up in and around the same blocks. It lost nearly half its people in a single decade, the 1960s, and its poverty rate is among the highest in Cleveland.

## 6. Counter-evidence and caveats

- **The narrowed area is small.** Two tracts with about 5,600 people. Tract 1093.01 has only 1,089 people, so ACS estimates there will have wide margins.
- **Rule tension.** Public housing is Central's core story, but the two tracts where one estate drives the numbers (Outhwaite, King Kennedy) are dropped. The story can tell their history while the charts use the two remaining tracts.
- **Hough and University-Euclid are next door, not in Central.** The 1966 uprising and University-Euclid displacement should be framed as citywide events that shaped Central's path, not as events in these tracts. Erieview was downtown.
- **Foreclosure is a weak chapter here.** Owner share is about 20% in our history file. The 2007 national spotlight was on Slavic Village. Check NEO CANDO before claiming Central was hit.
- **Source conflicts.** Hough uprising injuries: about 30 (ECH, "Hough Riots") vs. 50 (ECH, "Hough"). Hough population: 65,694 in 1950 as the peak (ECH) vs. "more than 66,000 in 1966" (Wikipedia). HUD unit counts differ from the triage note.
- **History numbers need a rerun.** history_long.csv covers all 8 candidate tracts, including Hough, Fairfax, Kinsman and Slavic Village tracts. It must be rebuilt for 1087.01 and 1093.01. Its 1970 total (61,788 for 8 tracts) cannot be compared with ECH's Central figure (27,280).
- **The series starts too late.** Our tract series begins in 1970, after Central's biggest drop (1960 to 1970). Use NHGIS tracts back to 1940 or 1950 for this chart.
- **Values that look off in history_long.csv (8 tracts):** poverty dips from 60.1% (1990) to 49.7% (2000) and then rises to 61.5% (ACS 2020-2024). Vacancy falls from 26.7% (ACS 2008-2012) to 13.8% (2020 census). That fall likely reflects demolition of vacant homes rather than recovery; confirm with Land Bank demolition counts. Unemployment above 33% in both ACS periods needs a margin-of-error check.
- **Job losses are county-level only.** I found no dated plant closing inside or next to Central with a job count.
