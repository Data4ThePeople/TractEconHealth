## 3. How the cuts compound

Austin was built around one cannery and the can plant next to it, and the can plant closed in 1986. The cannery kept going and even added jobs in the 1990s, but county factory jobs fell by almost half from 2000 to 2011, and by 2012 four in ten working residents drove out of the county for work. Before 2015, the town had no addiction treatment and no HIV clinic, and Scott County ranked last in Indiana for life expectancy, according to a 2016 medical review. Doctors writing for the CDC tied the outbreak to people crushing and injecting a pain pill, oxymorphone, and sharing needles in a state that banned needle exchange until the emergency order. The timing of the job losses and the spread of drug injection lines up, but the sources I found do not measure a direct link between them.

## 5. What makes it distinct

Austin is the one place in the series where an infectious disease outbreak marks the low point, and that outbreak is among the best documented public health events in rural America. The cannery never closed, so this is a story of a town that kept its main plant but still lost ground.

## 6. Counter-evidence and caveats

- **The cannery did not go dark.** County food jobs grew 90% from 1987 to 1995, and a food plant of 500 to 999 workers shows up in County Business Patterns size data from 2005 through 2015.
- **A possible late-1990s cut needs checking.** County food jobs were 2,468 in 1997, but in 2000 the county's largest food plant was in the 250-to-499-worker size class. Either food jobs fell sharply around 1998 or the switch from SIC to NAICS industry codes moved jobs to another category. UNCONFIRMED; QCEW or Indiana workforce data could settle it. County factory jobs in 2022 (2,544) are close to 2000 (2,830). The "factory town that went dark" framing does not fit the data I found. The decline is better told as the can plant closing, a 2000-2011 job drop, and long commutes.
- **The early cuts are thin.** Only the 1986 can plant closing has a date, and it has no job count. The Pepsi and Tri-Hawk cuts named in the triage notes have no source yet.
- **The triage notes cite a wrong link.** The Daily Herald link (https://www.dailyherald.com/?p=211301) is a 2013 opinion column about a coal plant, not Austin. The PMC link the notes call "Peters et al., NEJM" is a different paper (Janowicz, Topics in Antiviral Medicine, 2016).
- **Sources disagree on the old HIV baseline.** The MMWR says fewer than five cases a year; Janowicz says five in ten years.
- **Case counts change by date.** Use 135 (Apr 21, 2015), 188 (Apr 2016) or the final count from Peters et al., NEJM 2016 (https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1515195, would not load), and always give the date. The "215" figure in the brief is UNCONFIRMED.
- **Commuting samples are small**, and the 2018-2022 file shows zero out-of-state commuters where 2008-2012 showed 152, which is likely sampling noise.
- **Our history numbers look off in places:**
  - Owner share in ACS and the census disagree. I checked the census directly: 2010 owners were 864 of 1,568 homes (55.1%) and 2020 owners were 739 of 1,513 (48.8%). ACS gives 64.9% (2008-2012) and 65.3% (2020-2024). Do not chart ACS and census owner share on the same line.
  - Unemployment drops from 14.8% (1990) to 7.4% (2000) and back to 16.4% (2008-2012). Poverty goes 30.7%, 19.1%, 21.4%, 26.8%. The 2000 poverty rate matches the city figure (19.1%), but the 1990 values are high and the 1990 fit is 0.896.
  - Our tract shows 4,250 people in 2000 while the city shows 4,724, even though the tract is now 98% city. The city boundary or the tract fit likely changed. UNCONFIRMED.
