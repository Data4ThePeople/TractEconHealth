## 2. Timeline

1. **1900.** Coahoma County had 26,293 people. It had more than 2,600 sharecropping households, the second most in Mississippi, and only 235 of 3,797 Black farm households (6%) owned land. https://mississippiencyclopedia.org/entries/coahoma-county/ ; population https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coahoma_County,_Mississippi
2. **About 1920.** Cotton prices fell, and Black families began leaving. The Illinois Central depot in Clarksdale became "the primary departure point for many" headed to Chicago (Nicholas Lemann, *The Promised Land*, p. 6, as cited at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarksdale,_Mississippi ; book: https://books.google.com/books?id=FnlIhqBBEMkC).
3. **1930.** The county had about 46,300 people, and 94% of all farmers were tenants or sharecroppers. Industrial jobs (462) had not grown in 30 years. https://mississippiencyclopedia.org/entries/coahoma-county/
4. **1944.** Hopson Planting Company, just south of Clarksdale, grew what the Mississippi Blues Trail marker calls "the first crop of cotton to be entirely planted, harvested, and baled by machine." https://msbluestrail.org/?p=369 ; also https://eh.net/encyclopedia/mechanical-cotton-picker/ (Donald Holley). A Clarksdale city history gives 1946 for International Harvester's work on 28 acres at Hopson (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarksdale,_Mississippi). Use 1944 for the full crop.
5. **1949.** International Harvester made the first commercial cotton pickers. Most U.S. cotton was still picked by hand a decade later; machines picked nearly all of it by the late 1960s. https://eh.net/encyclopedia/mechanical-cotton-picker/
6. **1950.** County population peaked at 49,361. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coahoma_County,_Mississippi
7. **May 29, 1958, and 1960.** Martin Luther King Jr. came to Clarksdale for the first major SCLC meeting. In 1960 Clarksdale pharmacist Aaron Henry became state NAACP president and led a two-year boycott of Clarksdale businesses. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarksdale,_Mississippi (citing Morrison and Dittmer)
8. **1960.** The county had 46,212 people, 68% Black, and most workers were still in farming. The encyclopedia's description of this period adds that more than a third of residents had less than five years of schooling and the county ranked second in the state in public assistance payments. https://mississippiencyclopedia.org/entries/coahoma-county/
9. **April 10, 1967.** A Senate poverty subcommittee held a hearing in Jackson. Marian Wright testified that mechanization and federal rules requiring cotton land to "lie fallow" had put thousands of sharecroppers out of work. Senators Clark and Kennedy then toured the Delta, ending at the Clarksdale Neighborhood Center, where about 1,000 people waited. The Field Foundation doctors' report and a new Senate Select Committee on Nutrition and Human Needs followed. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_S._Clark%27s_and_Robert_F._Kennedy%27s_tour_of_the_Mississippi_Delta (the article is inconsistent on whether the tour was April 10 or 11)
10. **1970.** Clarksdale reached its peak population, 21,673. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarksdale,_Mississippi
11. **February 1 and 2, 1970.** After a Supreme Court ruling, Clarksdale's Black and white high schools merged. When schools reopened, 574 of 585 white students at Clarksdale Junior High did not show up. Lee Academy opened that year with 654 students. https://hechingerreport.org/a-charter-school-faces-the-ugly-history-of-school-choice-in-the-deep-south/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_Academy_(Mississippi)
12. **1980.** Farm jobs had fallen sharply, though the county still ranked fifth in the state in farm employment. Factory work was mostly food processing and fabricated metal. https://mississippiencyclopedia.org/entries/coahoma-county/ (no job counts given)
13. **1990 to 2020.** The five tracts fell from about 23,800 people to 15,850 (down 33%). Poverty was 43% in 1990 and 38% in 2020 to 2024. Vacant homes rose from 6% to 16%. County population fell from 31,665 to 21,390 (down 32%), and 57% from the 1950 peak. Project file `analysis/spotlight/history_long.csv` (NHGIS and ACS); county counts https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coahoma_County,_Mississippi
14. **2015.** Coahoma County's private schools were 7% Black, while its public school systems were more than 90% Black. Lee Academy was 75% white. https://hechingerreport.org/a-charter-school-faces-the-ugly-history-of-school-choice-in-the-deep-south/
15. **2020 to 2022 deaths.** County life expectancy was 65.3 years, against 71.9 for Mississippi and 77.1 for the U.S. County Health Rankings 2025 analytic file (project copy `data/raw/chr/analytic_data2025_v3.csv`), https://www.countyhealthrankings.org . The five tracts had a 2010 to 2015 life expectancy of 71.3 (USALEEP, `history_long.csv`).

## 3. How the cuts compound

By 1900 most Black farm families in Coahoma County worked land they did not own, and by 1930 almost every farmer was a tenant or sharecropper. When cotton prices fell around 1920, the Illinois Central line out of Clarksdale became a main route to Chicago (Lemann). The first all-machine cotton crop was grown at Hopson in 1944, and witnesses at the 1967 Senate hearing said machines and federal acreage rules had put thousands of sharecroppers out of work. Historian Donald Holley argues the arrow also ran the other way: people leaving for better wages pushed planters to buy machines. When the public schools fully integrated in 1970, nearly all white students moved to private academies, and in 2015 the county's private schools were still 7% Black while its public schools were more than 90% Black. The county peaked in 1950, the city peaked in 1970, and both have shrunk every decade since 2000; the timing lines up with each of these changes, but no single source ties the recent losses to one cause.

## 5. What makes it distinct

Clarksdale is where the first cotton crop grown and picked entirely by machine was raised (1944), and where the 1967 Senate hunger tour ended, so the end of sharecropping has a specific place and date here. Its schools also give one of the clearest dated records of the split into a mostly Black public system and a mostly white private academy in 1970.

## 6. Counter-evidence and caveats

- **Mechanization's role is debated.** Holley (https://eh.net/encyclopedia/mechanical-cotton-picker/) writes that "migration greatly accelerated mechanization" and that higher factory wages pulled workers north. He finds machines explain less than 40% of the drop in hand picking. Lemann's book leans the other way. The story should present both, not "the machine drove people out."
- **The 1944 versus 1946 date.** Most sources say 1944; one city history says 1946. Use 1944 and say it was the first full crop.
- **1930 county count.** Wikipedia gives 46,327 and the Mississippi Encyclopedia gives 46,237. Pull the NHGIS figure before publishing.
- **Recent poverty did not get worse.** Tract poverty fell from 43% (1990) to 38% (2020 to 2024). The decline story is clearer in population, vacancy and life expectancy than in the poverty rate.
- **Our history numbers.** The 1980 row (fit 0.774) is under the 0.8 cutoff and should not be used; it also conflicts with 1990 (74.7% Black in 1980 vs. 62.5% in 1990). The tract Black share jumps from 62% (1990) to 78% (2010), faster than the county (64% to 75%); check the 1990 crosswalk before charting. Unemployment of 18.6% in 2008 to 2012 is an ACS five-year figure around the recession and has wide error bars.
- **City versus tracts.** The city grew from 19,717 (1990) to 20,645 (2000) while the tracts shrank. That may reflect annexation; the city series is not a clean comparison. Use tracts or the county.
- **"Micro," not rural.** The candidate file codes Clarksdale as a micropolitan small city (RUCA 4 and 10). If the series needs 5 strictly rural places, this counts as small-town, not rural.
- **Gaps.** Plant closures, hospital history and casino jobs are unconfirmed. These are the "middle decades" cuts (1970s to 2000s) the series promise needs, and they will take local newspaper work (Clarksdale Press Register archive).
