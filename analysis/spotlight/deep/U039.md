# U039 deep dive: West Baltimore (Sandtown-Winchester, Harlem Park, Upton, Franklin Square, Poppleton), Baltimore, MD

Prepared 2026-11-19. Each fact below was checked against the URL given with it, unless it is marked UNCONFIRMED. ## 2. Timeline

1. **1910.** Baltimore becomes "the first city in America to pass an ordinance establishing block-by-block segregation" (U.S. Department of Justice, Investigation of the Baltimore City Police Department, August 10, 2016, p. 12: https://www.justice.gov/crt/file/883296/download). The City of Baltimore's 2024 federal grant application for the US 40 corridor makes the same point about "the communities surrounding US 40" (p. 8: https://www.mdot.maryland.gov/OPCP/Narrative_2024_West_Baltimore_United_Phase_1_RCP_Construction.pdf). The ordinance details (signing date, the 1911 and 1913 versions, the Maryland court rulings) need Garrett Power, "Apartheid Baltimore Style: The Residential Segregation Ordinances of 1910-1913," 42 Md. L. Rev. 289 (1983): https://digitalcommons.law.umaryland.edu/mlr/vol42/iss2/4/ (PDF blocked to us; for Eric to pull).
2. **November 5, 1917.** In Buchanan v. Warley, 245 U.S. 60, the Supreme Court strikes down Louisville's racial zoning law: "the ordinance cannot stand" (https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/245/60). The DOJ report (p. 72, note 79) says "the effect in Baltimore was minimal": owners kept the rule going through racial covenants, and the mayor had inspectors cite anyone who sold or rented to Black families in white areas, citing Antero Pietila, Not in My Neighborhood (2010), pp. 35, 53-54.
3. **May 1, 1937.** Federal officials issue the Residential Security Map of Baltimore, which grades neighborhoods A to D with D "characterized by detrimental influences in a pronounced degree, undesirable population or an infiltration of it." Thompson v. HUD, 348 F. Supp. 2d 398 (D. Md. 2005), supplemental findings: https://hallapproved.com/md/cases/federal/district/2005/2306838/ . Our overlay of the Mapping Inequality polygons (https://dsl.richmond.edu/panorama/redlining/) on the 2020 tracts: 87.5% of the area of the eight tracts is grade D, all of it in area D4. Seven tracts are 98% to 100% D; tract 1604 (south Harlem Park) is 99.9% C (areas C6 and C7). The D4 area description text is UNCONFIRMED.
4. **1937 to 1943.** The Housing Authority of Baltimore City builds eight "de jure segregated" projects; five, including Gilmor Homes, are designated "Negro housing" and placed "in existing African-American neighborhoods" (Thompson v. HUD, supplemental findings, above). Gilmor Homes is in tract 1501 (our geocode).
5. **1952 and 1997.** The City Council approves the site for Murphy Homes in 1952; housing authority papers list it as "Non-White" (Thompson v. HUD, above). Its high-rises stood at 1058 Argyle Ave, tract 1703 (our geocode). In 1997 HUD gives the housing authority "a $31.3 million HOPE VI revitalization grant for Murphy Homes and Julian Gardens" (HUD news release 99-182, September 7, 1999: https://archives.hud.gov/news/1999/pr99-182.html). The July 1999 implosion date is UNCONFIRMED (for Eric to pull).
6. **October 1965.** The housing authority's first scattered-site project plans 100 public housing units in the Harlem Park urban renewal area. All of the families relocated to make way for it were African American, and all units went into tracts that were over 90% Black in 1970 (Thompson v. HUD, above). The 1959 Harlem Park urban renewal plan (Baltimore Urban Renewal and Housing Agency) is listed in a Cornell archive but could not be opened.
7. **1966 to 1979.** Condemnations for the Franklin-Mulberry expressway begin in 1966, and the road opens on February 5, 1979 (Ron Cassie, Baltimore Magazine, February 2023: https://www.baltimoremagazine.com/section/businessdevelopment/highway-to-nowhere-baltimore-expressway-demolished-black-neighborhoods/). The City of Baltimore says "971 homes, 62 businesses, one school, and 20 city blocks were demolished and an estimated 1,500 residents were displaced" for the 1.4-mile road, which "was intended to extend another three and a half miles west to I-70" but "was never completed" (2024 grant application, pp. 2-3, above). It names Harlem Park, Franklin Square and Poppleton among the Black neighborhoods hit. Baltimore Magazine gives "more than 2,800 people" displaced; the two counts differ.
8. **April 6 to 12, 1968.** After the King assassination, looting begins "on Pennsylvania Ave. in the 1200-2000 blocks" on April 6 and hits "Gilmore Ave., from Baltimore to Franklin" on April 7. Citywide: six deaths, more than 5,700 arrests, 1,208 fires (University of Baltimore, Baltimore '68 events timeline: https://archives.ubalt.edu/bsr/timeline.htm). The 1200-1300 blocks of Pennsylvania Ave are in tract 1703, and N Gilmor St between Baltimore and Franklin is in tract 1901 (our geocode).
9. **Late 1980s to 2000.** Mayor Kurt Schmoke, the community group BUILD and the Enterprise Foundation start the Sandtown-Winchester Neighborhood Transformation Initiative, which "raised more than $130 million" through 2000; almost 600 Gilmor Homes units are modernized in the early 1990s (Stefanie DeLuca and Peter Rosenblatt, The Abell Report, November 2013: https://abell.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/arn1113.pdf). The same authors report homeownership rose from under a quarter to over 40% of families, with no effect on long-term poverty (Urban Affairs Review blog, November 16, 2015: https://urbanaffairsreview.blogspot.com/2015/11/authors-blog-what-happened-in-sandtown.html).
10. **January 6, 2005.** Judge Garbis (U.S. District Court for Maryland) rules in Thompson v. HUD that HUD "failed to consider regionally-oriented desegregation and integration policies." The findings count 302 scattered-site public housing units in Sandtown-Winchester, 227 in Harlem Park, 173 in Franklin Square and 116 in Upton, and note that in 1979 HUD's fair housing office recommended against placing an assisted housing project in Upton because of its "high minority and low-income concentration" (above).
11. **February 2015.** The Justice Policy Institute and Prison Policy Initiative count 458 people in prison from the Sandtown-Winchester/Harlem Park area (2010 data), 3,075 per 100,000 residents, at a state cost of $16,946,000 a year, the most of any Baltimore community (The Right Investment?: https://static.prisonpolicy.org/origin/md/report.pdf ; report page: https://www.prisonpolicy.org/origin/md/report.html).
12. **April 12 and 19, 2015.** "On April 12, 2015, Freddie Gray suffered an injury while in police custody; one week later, he died from those injuries" (Maryland Court of Appeals, State v. Rice, May 20, 2016: https://cases.justia.com/maryland/court-of-appeals/2016-96-15-0.pdf). His arrest was on Presbury Street (Mark Reutter, Baltimore Brew, May 1, 2015: https://baltimorebrew.com/2015/05/01/freddie-grays-death-ruled-a-homicide-six-officers-charged/). The 1600 block of Presbury is in tract 1501; the 1700 block, given in many reports, is in tract 1502, just outside our set (our geocode).
13. **June 25, 2015.** Gov. Larry Hogan cancels the Red Line, an east-west light rail line with "a $900 million commitment" in federal money (Baltimore Magazine, June 25, 2015: https://www.baltimoremagazine.com/2015/6/25/hogan-says-no-to-red-line). On December 18, 2015, the NAACP Legal Defense Fund and others file a Title VI civil rights complaint over the cancellation (https://www.naacpldf.org/press-release/baltimore-residents-and-civic-groups-file-title-vi-complaint-with-united-states-department-of-transportation-over-marylands-discriminatory-decision-to-strip-baltimore-of-transportation-fundin/). The state relaunches the project in summer 2023 and picks light rail in summer 2024 (Maryland Transit Administration: https://redlinemaryland.com). The city's 2024 application plans a "Harlem Park Red Line light rail station" on the highway corridor (above).
14. **August 10, 2016.** The Justice Department finds that police "made roughly 44 percent of its stops in two small, predominantly African-American districts that contain only 11 percent of the City's population," and that one man was stopped 34 times (DOJ report, pp. 7 and 27, above). It also reports Sandtown-Winchester/Harlem Park had the city's highest share of children with elevated blood lead, 7.4% (p. 15). The city and DOJ file a consent decree on January 12, 2017, and the federal court enters it on April 7, 2017 (U.S. District Court for Maryland: https://www.mdd.uscourts.gov/Baltimore-City-Consent-Decree).
15. **June 2017.** The Baltimore City Health Department's neighborhood profiles give life expectancy at birth (2011-2015 deaths) of 70.0 years in Sandtown-Winchester/Harlem Park, 68.2 in Upton/Druid Heights and 68.4 in Poppleton/The Terraces/Hollins Market, against 73.6 for the city. Vacant building density in Sandtown-Winchester/Harlem Park is 2,560 per 10,000 housing units, against 562 citywide (2016). The 2011 profiles (2005-2009 deaths) gave 65.3, 62.9 and 64.0 years, against 71.8 citywide. Profiles: https://www.baltimorecity.gov/media/document/nhp201747sandtown-winchester-harlemparkrev6-9-17pdf ; https://www.baltimorecity.gov/media/document/nhp201753upton-druidheightsrev6-9-17pdf ; https://www.baltimorecity.gov/media/document/nhp201746poppleton-theterraces-hollinsmarketrev6-9-17pdf ; 2011: https://s3.amazonaws.com/baltimorecity.gov.if-us-east-1/s3fs-public/2025-05/47_sandtown.pdf
16. **2023 and 2024.** Baltimore receives a $2 million federal Reconnecting Communities planning grant for the highway corridor (Sen. Van Hollen release, April 25, 2023: https://www.vanhollen.senate.gov/news/press-releases/maryland-congressional-members-mayor-scott-highlight-federal-funding-to-move-forward-with-highway-to-nowhere-redevelopment). In 2024 the city asks for $100 million toward a $200 million first phase to cap part of the trench (2024 application, p. 1, above). Whether that request was funded is UNCONFIRMED.

**Check against our census numbers (history_final.csv, fit 0.8 or more):** population fell from 40,903 (1970) to 32,896 (1980), 31,601 (1990), 22,221 (2000), 19,910 (2010) and 15,991 (2020), a 61% drop. The 1970s drop of 20% matches the years of highway clearance. Unemployment went from 8.5% (1970) to 22.3% (1980), and stayed between 18% and 25% through 2008-2012 before falling to 13.1% (2020-2024). Poverty was 41.5% in 1970, about 50% in 1980 and 1990, and 39.3% in 2020-2024. Vacancy rose from 17.4% (1990) to 33.0% (2008-2012) and was 21.9% in 2020-2024. Black residents were 98.8% of the population in 1970 and 89.8% in 2020-2024. Life expectancy (USALEEP 2010-2015): 69.1 years.

## 3. How the cuts compound

The city's own 2024 grant application draws the line from the 1910 ordinance through racial covenants and the 1937 map to today: residents of Black neighborhoods "like those in the US 40 corridor could not access credit," and "these patterns of red lining are echoed in today's maps of poverty, physical disrepair, crime, housing values, homeownership, and income." A federal court found that the housing authority placed its earliest public housing for Black families (1937 to 1943), including Gilmor Homes, inside existing Black neighborhoods, and later put scattered-site units in Sandtown, Harlem Park, Franklin Square and Upton; it found that the majority of scattered-site units "are located in high poverty Black neighborhoods." The city says the highway took 971 homes and 62 businesses and that West Baltimore communities "have continued to face blight and disinvestment in the decades since." Our census numbers show the neighborhood lost 20% of its people in the 1970s, the decade of highway clearance; the timing lines up, but no source here measures how much of that loss the road caused. The Justice Policy Institute reports that the community with the most people in prison also faces the most concentrated problems with work, education, health and housing, but it does not claim one causes the other. The Health Department reports life expectancy several years below the city's in all three areas.

## 5. What makes it distinct

Here a federal court's findings name the public housing projects and neighborhoods one by one, so the public housing layer of the story rests on a court record, not on later summaries. On top of that, the city itself documents the highway's toll (971 homes, 62 businesses), the Justice Department documents the policing, and the Health Department publishes life expectancy for these same neighborhoods three times over 10 years.

## 6. Counter-evidence and caveats

- **CSA vs. tract geography.** The life expectancy, incarceration, lead and vacancy figures from the Health Department, JPI and DOJ cover Community Statistical Areas that are larger than our tracts. The Sandtown-Winchester/Harlem Park CSA includes tract 1502, which is not in our set. Label these as neighborhood-area figures.
- **Freddie Gray's arrest site.** Most reports put the arrest in the 1700 block of Presbury St, which is in tract 1502, one block outside our set. Gilmor Homes, where the encounter is widely reported to have started, is in tract 1501. Say "in Sandtown" and avoid saying the arrest happened inside our tracts.
- **Harlem Park was graded C, not D.** Tract 1604 is almost entirely grade C. The story should not say every tract was redlined.
- **Highway numbers differ.** The city says about 1,500 residents displaced; Baltimore Magazine says more than 2,800. The 971 homes and 62 businesses count is the same in both, but its original source (likely a 1970s state or city relocation report) is not in hand.
- **The 1970s drop has many causes.** Population fell in much of Baltimore in the 1970s. The highway, the 1968 unrest and suburban moves all overlap, and no source here separates them.
- **Life expectancy has risen.** Health Department figures rose about 5 years in each area between the 2011 and 2017 profiles, and BNIA's series shows small declines after 2015. Do not describe health as only getting worse.
- **Investment did not stop.** The Sandtown initiative put more than $130 million into the area, and the Abell authors found it likely raised homeownership. The story should present it as a large effort with mixed results, not as neglect.
- **Blockbusting and contract buying.** The DOJ report lists "a contract system for housing loans" in Baltimore's history, citing Pietila. No source opened for this note ties blockbusting or contract sales to these tracts. Keep it as citywide context unless Pietila's book gives a local example.
- **The 1937 Hoyt / real property survey** was not found online. Thompson v. HUD lists the HOLC "Explanation of Residential Security Map, Baltimore" and "Real Estate Situation" as trial exhibits, which may be the documents Eric has in mind.
- **Heavy national coverage.** Sandtown is one of the most written-about neighborhoods in the country since 2015. The fresh angle is the court record and the tract-level redlining overlay, not the 2015 events.
- **Red Line status.** The project was relaunched in 2023, but no construction date is confirmed. Do not say it will be built.
