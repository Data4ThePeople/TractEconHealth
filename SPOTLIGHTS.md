# Community spotlight series: the top 10 for approval

Step 1 working file, 2026-11-19. Approve or reject each pick by number. Backups are lettered A-H.

**How these were found.**
- **Screen:** 4,053 neighborhoods (clusters of adjacent census tracts) sit in the bottom 20% nationally on both median household income and our CDC health index. From those, we screened 131 candidates: the deepest in each state, plus the seeds you named.
- **Triage:** research agents wrote a first history of all 131.
- **Deep research:** 20 finalists got a second, deeper round. That round produced a dated, sourced timeline and a cleaned tract list for each one, with prisons, jails, single public housing complexes and tracts from other neighborhoods dropped.
- **Numbers:** every number in the tables below comes from our own pulls (NHGIS, ACS, USALEEP, County Health Rankings), not from the agents.
- **Sources:** full timelines with source links are in `analysis/spotlight/deep/<id>.md`.

**How to read the tables.**
- **Years shown:** a year appears only where the old census tracts match today's outline at 80% or better.
- **Sources by year:** 1970-2000 counts are from the census; 2010 is the census; 2020-2024 is the ACS five-year survey.
- **Blank 2010 cells:** poverty and unemployment for 2010 are not shown because that census did not ask about income or jobs.
- **Life expectancy:** USALEEP 2010-2015 tract estimates; the U.S. figure was 78.7. County life expectancy is County Health Rankings, 2021-2023.
- **Percentiles:** "Income percentile" and "health percentile" are the median tract's national rank today (0 is the lowest).

## Week 1: urban

### 1. West Dayton, Ohio (U047)

**Tracts:**
- Six tracts: 39113165100, 39113003800, 39113003900, 39113004100, 39113000200 and 39113070300.
- **Dropped:** Drexel (outside the city and the 1930s maps, and the healthiest tract in the cluster) and a tract that is 99% Trotwood.

**Where it ranks today:** income percentile 2.0; health percentile 1.1.

**Summary.** West Dayton is the series anchor because it shows nearly every kind of cut on one timeline.
- **1930s:** the federal mortgage maps graded its Black west side as a risk. Tract 1651 is 77% grade D.
- **1966:** unrest broke out on West Fifth Street.
- **US-35:** the freeway took 283 homes on one 1.4-mile stretch and split the area.
- **2008-09:** Delphi, GM and NCR left the region.
- **Overdoses:** the county overdose death rate rose from 22 to 82 per 100,000 at its peak.
- **2018:** Good Samaritan Hospital closed.

The neighborhood has 74% fewer people than in 1970. Poverty rose from 18% to 42%, and the share of homes owned by the people living in them fell from 55% to 34% after 1990.

**Main causes:** redlining; highway clearance; factory exits; overdoses; hospital closing.

### 2. Englewood and Fuller Park, Chicago (U061)

**Tracts:**
- Seven tracts: 17031680900, 17031681000, 17031681200, 17031681300, 17031834600, 17031835500 and 17031835600.
- **Dropped:** the Wentworth Gardens tract. Public housing holds 84% of its residents, and it is in Armour Square.

**Where it ranks today:** income percentile 0.9; health percentile 4.2.

**Summary.** The story here is a neighborhood that lost its jobs, its main shopping street and finally its schools.
- **1930s:** both areas were graded D almost wall to wall.
- **1961-62:** the Dan Ryan Expressway opened, splitting Fuller Park.
- **1971:** the Union Stock Yards next door closed.
- **1969-2004:** no new housing went up in Fuller Park.
- **After 1978:** Englewood's 63rd and Halsted shopping hub lost its anchor stores.
- **2018:** four high schools closed.

The two areas have 73% fewer people than in 1970. Unemployment went from 8% to 25%.

**Main causes:** packing-house jobs lost; expressway; retail collapse; disinvestment in housing and schools.

### 3. North Birmingham, Alabama (U034)

**Tracts:**
- Four tracts: 01073000500, 01073000700, 01073000800 and 01073005500 (Collegeville, Harriman Park, Fairmont).
- **Dropped:** a state prison tract, the county jail tract and two tracts in other neighborhoods.

**Where it ranks today:** income percentile 1.9; health percentile 2.3.

**Summary.** This is the pollution story. Worker housing went up beside coke ovens and pipe plants, and the plants are still inside the tract lines.
- Convict labor in the mines, racial zoning and grade D maps shaped who lived here.
- Steel and pipe jobs then left, but the plants that remained kept running.
- EPA found arsenic, lead and benzo(a)pyrene in hundreds of yards. It proposed a Superfund listing that never became final.
- In 2018 a federal jury convicted a lawyer and a coal company executive of bribing a state legislator to oppose that cleanup.

The area has 67% fewer people than in 1980. Poverty is 49%, and life expectancy is 67.5 years, the lowest of the 10.

**Main causes:** industrial pollution next to homes; segregation by law; steel job losses; a cleanup that stalled.

### 4. North Side Milwaukee, ZIP 53206 (U042)

**Tracts:**
- Four tracts: 55079004600, 55079004700, 55079006500 and 55079008800.
- **Dropped:** four tracts that are not in ZIP 53206.

**Where it ranks today:** income percentile 1.3; health percentile 1.1.

**Summary.** This is the jobs-and-prison story.
- **1930s:** the area was white and working class, and was graded "declining" (mostly C, not D).
- **1960s:** freeway clearance in Bronzeville pushed Black families north into it. It went from 72% Black in 1970 to 95% in 1980.
- **A.O. Smith:** the car frame plant stood inside tract 4700. It fell from a reported 10,000 jobs to about 300 when it closed in 2006.
- **Prison:** UW-Milwaukee researchers later called the ZIP "ground zero" for the imprisonment of Black men in Wisconsin.

Poverty rose from 20% to 38%, and owners fell from 43% to 31% after 1990.

**Main causes:** freeway displacement; a factory inside the neighborhood closing; mass incarceration.

### 5. Seventh Ward, St. Roch and Desire, New Orleans (U012)

**Tracts:**
- Six tracts: 22071001401, 22071002200, 22071002300, 22071002800, 22071003000 and 22071013702.
- **Dropped:** Venetian Isles and Pines Village, both in New Orleans East.

**Where it ranks today:** income percentile 0.7; health percentile 0.3.

**Summary.** This is a story of being walled in one layer at a time, long before Katrina.
- **1923:** the Industrial Canal cut the area off on one side.
- **1930s:** the federal mortgage maps graded it D.
- **1946 and 1956:** the Florida and Desire public housing projects went up on swamp and dump land.
- **Agriculture Street:** homes and a school were built on top of the old dump.
- **1966-68:** I-10 was raised over Claiborne Avenue, the Black business street.

The area had already lost 44% of its people between 1970 and 2000, before the storm. It then lost half of what was left by 2010.

**Main causes:** canal and highway barriers; public housing on waste land; homes on a landfill; flood.

### 6. Clarksdale, Mississippi Delta (M016)

**Tracts:**
- Five tracts: 28027950300, 28027950400, 28027950500, 28027950600 and 28027950700.
- Nothing was dropped.

**Where it ranks today:** income percentile 5.8; health percentile 7.6.

**Summary.** This is the Delta story: the end of sharecropping and a very long decline.
- **1930:** 94% of Coahoma County's farmers were tenants.
- **1944:** the first cotton crop grown and picked entirely by machine came from Hopson Plantation, just south of town.
- **1967:** the Senate hunger tour ended here.
- **1970:** white students left for Lee Academy as the schools desegregated.

The county has lost 57% of its people since 1950. These tracts lost 36% since 1990. County life expectancy is 65.3 years, 19th lowest of 2,870 counties in our data.

**Main causes:** sharecropping; farm mechanization; out-migration; school segregation after desegregation.

### 7. McDowell County coalfields, West Virginia (R007)

**Tracts:**
- Five tracts: 54047953600, 54047953800, 54047954200, 54047954501 and 54047954504.
- **Dropped:** the federal prison tract (1,881 of its 4,755 residents are inmates) and two Virginia tracts.

**Where it ranks today:** income percentile 2.9; health percentile 0.2.

**Summary.** McDowell was once the top coal-producing county in the U.S. It fell from 98,887 people in 1950 to 19,111 in 2020, an 81% drop. The cuts came one after another, and each has a county data series:
- Mine mechanization in the 1950s.
- U.S. Steel leaving its company town of Gary.
- Floods in 2001 and 2002.
- The pill wave, about 96 pain pills per resident per year from 2006 to 2012.
- The Walmart in Kimball closing in 2016.

County life expectancy is 65.5 years. Its overdose death rate is 193.8 per 100,000, the highest of any county in this list.

**Main causes:** a one-industry county; mechanization; corporate exit; floods; opioids; lost retail.

### 8. Pine Ridge Reservation, Oglala Lakota Nation, South Dakota (R046)

**Tracts:**
- Five tracts: 46102941000, 46102941100, 46102941200, 46071941200 and 46007941200.
- **Dropped:** three Rosebud tracts, home of the Sicangu Lakota, a separate nation.
- **Added:** Kyle, so the tracts cover all of Oglala Lakota County.

**Where it ranks today:** income percentile 9.5; health percentile 2.8.

**Summary.** This is the land-and-treaty story. Oglala Lakota land shrank in dated legal steps:
- The 1868 treaty.
- The 1877 act taking the Black Hills. In 1980 the Supreme Court ruled the Sioux Nation was owed compensation for it.
- The 1889 breakup of the Great Sioux Reservation.
- The 1910 opening of Bennett County.

Underfunded Indian Health Service care and alcohol sales in Whiteclay, Nebraska, just over the line until 2017, came later. Tribal colleges, health and land programs have pushed back. Oglala Lakota County's life expectancy is 58.4 years, third lowest of 2,870 counties in our data. Poverty in these tracts is 55%.

**Main causes:** treaty land loss; allotment; underfunded federal health care; alcohol sales at the border.

- The census undercounts reservations (5.64% in 2020).
- Its population grew, which sets it apart from the other nine.
- The story should be written with care and, ideally, with Oglala Lakota voices.

### 9. Rocky Ford, Colorado (R031)

**Tract:** 08089968100, the north 61% of town. The south side is in a tract that does not pass the screen.

**Where it ranks today:** income percentile 4.0; health percentile 3.9.

**Summary.** This is the farm-town story: a melon and sugar beet town on the Arkansas River.
- **1974:** federal sugar quotas ended.
- **1979:** the sugar plant closed.
- **1980s:** the town's ditch water was sold to the city of Aurora, and about 7,500 acres of farmland dried up, according to the local water district.
- **1987-1994:** food processing jobs in the county fell by about half.

Today, 35% of residents live in poverty, and 27% of homes are vacant, up from 16% in 1990.

**Main causes:** the end of a crop program; plant closing; farm water sold to a city; the 1980s farm crisis.

### 10. Owsley County, Kentucky (R004)

**Tracts:**
- Both of the county's tracts: 21189930100 and 21189930200.
- Together they are the whole county, so tract and county data describe the same place.

**Where it ranks today:** both tracts pass the screen using CDC PLACES 2024. The 2025 release left out Kentucky.

**Summary.** This is the tobacco story. Small burley tobacco farms and coal wages earned by commuting to nearby counties carried Owsley County for decades.
- After 1940, the county lost 55% of its people.
- In 2004, the federal tobacco quota program ended with a buyout. The county then reported no tobacco, 84 farms and farm income below zero.
- Poverty has stayed above 30% for 35 years; it is 36% today.
- Life expectancy differs by 11 years between the county's two tracts: 77.3 and 66.5.

**Main causes:** the loss of a federal crop program; small farms; a long population drain.

## Backups

**Urban**
- **A. Nubian Square, Roxbury, Boston (tracts 25025080300, 25025080401).**
  - Why it's here: tract 803 is the one in the city health department's headline gap, 68.8 years against 91.6 in Back Bay (2015-2021). Its cuts are dated public actions: grade D maps, Inner Belt clearance, urban renewal, the elevated train removed in 1987 and the housing rebuild.
  - Why it's a backup: about two-thirds of homes are subsidized, so low income partly reflects income limits on that housing. USALEEP puts the two tracts at 77.6 years.
  - Grove Hall, the first Boston pick, is weaker: its gap to Back Bay is only 1.4 to 4.6 years.
- **B. El Segundo Barrio and Chamizal, El Paso (6 tracts).**
  - Why it's here: the city took land and homes again and again. The 1916 cleanup razed more than 100 homes. The Paisano Drive widening (1946-47) displaced up to 6,000 people. The Chamizal border change (1963-67) displaced more than 5,600. Then El Paso lost 92% of its apparel jobs, 1990-2019. Every graded block was D.
  - Why it's a backup: tract life expectancy is 76.7 years.
- **C. North-central and east Flint (8 tracts).**
  - Why it's here: the 1965-77 St. John Street clearance for I-475 and Buick City, Buick City's 1999 closing, two state takeovers of city finances, then the 2014 water switch. These tracts lost half their people from 1990 to 2020.
  - Why it's a backup: the St. John core sits mostly outside these tracts, and the GM job counts need primary sources.
- **D. West Baltimore (U039, 8 tracts).**
  - Why it's here: strong numbers. 59% fewer people since 1970, life expectancy 69.1.
  - Why it's a backup: it had triage only, no deep research yet.

**Rural**
- **E. Helena-West Helena and Elaine, Arkansas Delta (7 tracts).**
  - Why it's here: the 1919 Elaine massacre of organizing sharecroppers, then the steepest population loss of any U.S. metro or micropolitan area in the 2000s. Life expectancy is 70.8 for the tracts and 67.0 for the county.
  - Why it's a backup: it would repeat the Delta story.
- **F. Black Mesa, Navajo Nation, Arizona (5 tracts).**
  - Why it's here: fifty years of coal, the federal land partition that relocated more than 3,600 families, mine closings in 2005 and 2019, and 25% of homes without complete plumbing.
  - Why it's a backup: it would repeat both the tribal and the coal stories, and it has no matching health series.
- **G. Uptown Butte, Montana (2 tracts).**
  - Why it's here: the company town lost Anaconda, its mines, its smelter and whole neighborhoods to the Berkeley Pit. The lead cleanup grew again in 2026.
  - Why it's a backup: drawn to match its old tract, it sits at the 27th percentile on health, so it no longer passes the screen.
- **H. Harlan County coal camps, Kentucky.**
  - Why it's here: a second coal story.
  - Why it's a backup: McDowell's decline is deeper (81% vs. 64%) and better documented.
