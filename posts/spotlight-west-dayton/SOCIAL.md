In 1937, a federal loan map graded parts of West Dayton "hazardous."
Ninety years later, 42% of the people who live there are in poverty, up from 18% in 1970.

Today we start a new series, Stories of a Forgotten American Community. We picked 10 places out of thousands across the country with low incomes and poor health, five in cities and five in rural areas, and went back through the past century to see how each one got here.

The first is West Dayton, Ohio. Its story is a series of hits that built on each other:

- A 1937 federal loan map. Of the Black residents who lived anywhere on Dayton's graded map, 97% lived in the areas graded D.
- Unrest in 1966 and two freeways through the west side.
- People leaving. The six census tracts held 49,314 people in 1970 and about 12,900 now, 74% fewer.
- Factory jobs going away. Residents held 1,076 manufacturing jobs in 2002 and 415 in 2010.
- The neighborhood Kroger closing in 2008. Today there is no full grocery store inside the six tracts, and 21% of households have no vehicle.

Today 29% of adults here have diabetes, compared with 12% nationwide.

As you read, ask yourself one question: What would your life be like if you had grown up here?
